For three months I lived as a monk in a monastery in Chiang Mai,
Thailand. Head shaved, robes on, alms bowl in hand before dawn — the
full form of a life that has been lived the same way for over two
thousand years.

## Ordination

Temporary ordination is a living part of Thai Buddhist culture — men
ordain for a season, a rains retreat, or a stretch of months, and the
door back to lay life is open and honored. The ordination itself is a
ceremony of requests: asking for the going-forth, receiving the
robes, taking the precepts, and being given a new name in Pali.

## The monastery day

The day begins in the dark. Morning chanting, then the alms round —
walking barefoot through the streets as the town wakes, receiving
food from householders who kneel to give it. The one meal eaten
before noon. Then the day's real work: sitting meditation, walking
meditation, sweeping the grounds, study, the small labors of keeping
a monastery alive. Evening chanting closes the day, and the night is
quiet early.

The form is strict and the strictness is the point. With every choice
about food, clothing, money, and time already made by the discipline,
what remains is the mind — met hour after hour with nothing to
distract it and nowhere else to go.

## What comes home

The robes come off, the hair grows back, and lay life resumes with
all its choices restored. What remains is what the form revealed —
how little is actually needed, what the mind does when it is finally
watched, and a gratitude to the lineage of people who have kept this
way of living intact so that anyone sincere can step into it, even
for only three months.

## Key Takeaways

- Temporary ordination is a living tradition in Thailand, and the door into monastic life is genuinely open.
- The monastic form makes every daily choice in advance so that attention can turn fully to the mind.
- What comes home from three months in robes is not the form but what the form revealed.
