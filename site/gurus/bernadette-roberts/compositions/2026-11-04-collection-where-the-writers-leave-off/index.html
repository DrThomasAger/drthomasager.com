Where the Writers Leave Off

Collection on a topicno-self2026-11-04 · composed with claude-opus-5-5 from the verified quotationsEdit this page

Bernadette Roberts (1931–2017) was a Christian contemplative who described two movements in the inner life. The first is the familiar one of the mystics, the loss of the ego as the self comes to rest in union with God. The second, which she lived and wrote about in The Experience of No-Self (1982) and the books after it, is the falling away of that unitive self as well, and the seeing of what remains when there is no self at all. This short study gathers eight brief passages from her books and an interview on that second movement, each followed by a few lines of commentary. Her words are under copyright, so they are quoted sparingly and each is given with its source.

The moment

the moment was unheralded, unrecognized, and unknown; it was the moment "I" entered a great silence and never returned

Mystics, Masters, Saints and Sages (2001), her autobiographical sketch, Part I: The Journey, ch. 1

She places the turning point outside any event she could have noticed or prepared for. Nothing announced it, and nothing in her recognised it at the time. The silence she names is not a state she entered and left, since by her account the one who could return was no longer there.

Instead of the usual unlocalized centre of myself, there was nothing there, it was empty, and at the moment of seeing this there was a flood of quiet joy.

The Experience of No-Self, Part I

Here the absence is seen directly. The centre she had always lived from, which was never located anywhere in particular, is found to be empty. The joy she reports is quiet, and it comes with the seeing rather than as a reward for it.

What remained

In the absence of self, all that remained was an immovable stillness, an unbreakable, unfeeling silence.

Mystics, Masters, Saints and Sages (2001), her autobiographical sketch

The words immovable, unbreakable and unfeeling describe something that does not respond the way a self responds. She is careful not to make this stillness into a new and better self. It is what is left, described only by what it lacks.

When there is no longer a cyclone, there is no longer an eye.

interview: Stephan Bodian 1986

Her image turns a common teaching around. Many traditions speak of a calm centre within the storm of thought and feeling. She says that when the storm ends, the calm centre ends with it, because the centre only existed in relation to what moved around it.

The self as a passing function

Self is not our true life or our real nature; it is but a temporary mechanism

What Is Self?

This is the root of her difference from much of the contemplative literature. The self is not something to be purified and kept, but a mechanism with a time and a purpose. When its purpose is fulfilled, it can fall away.

No-self, then, means no-consciousness.

interview: Stephan Bodian 1986

She uses self and consciousness as names for one and the same experience. So the end of self, for her, is not a deeper or wider consciousness. It is the end of the reflexive knowing that consciousness is.

Beyond the unitive state

The assumption that the egoless condition, or union of self and God, is man's final goal and ultimate destiny is a great mistake.

The Path to No-Self, Preface

Her books draw this line again and again. The union of self and God, which most mystical writing treats as the summit, is for her a stage that is lived and then passed through. Her own writing begins where she found that union itself giving way.

… This is not a journey for those who expect love and bliss.

The Experience of No-Self, Introduction

She gives this as a direct warning to the reader. The passage she describes is one of acclimatising to a new way of seeing, often bewildering and sometimes frightening, before anything settles. What she offers is an account of what happened to her, not a promise of a pleasant state.
